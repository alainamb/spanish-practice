Definite Articles - Using the

Introduction

The definite article ("the" in English) is used to indicate a specific noun (el libro), as opposed to the indefinite article ("a" or "an" in English), which is used to refer in general to a noun (un libro). This building block of language differs in how we use it in English in that articles must match the nouns they modify in gender and number.

While the overall concept of definite articles is similar between Englisn and Spanish, actual usage differs. Spanish tends to require a definite article more frequently than in English, though the definite article is not always necessary. This lesson teaches when to use the definite article in Spanish, and when it can be omitted. The lesson also covers the non-binary forms of the definite article for those who are interested, though these are not yet accepted as standard.

Reference Guide

Definite articles

Definite articles must match the noun they modify in gender and number. Counting the non-binary articles, this means that there are six definite articles in Spanish, found in the table below. Nouns that are inanimate will take the masculine or feminine article depending on their gender. Nouns that refer to people will take the appropriate article based on their gender. All nouns must also be modified by the definite articles that reflects whether the noun is singular or plural.

Masculine Feminine Non-binary
Singular el la le
Plural los las les

Note: The singular, masculine definite article "el" does not use an accent. The word "él" (with an accent) means "he" or "him". For example: El libro es para él (The book is for him).

When and when not to use the definite article

As indicated in the introduction to this lesson, Spanish requires the definite article much more frequently than English does. However, there are some cases where the definite article can be omitted. You can learn more about each of these case (when to use, when to omit) here.

When to use the definite article

Here are some contexts where Spanish requires the definite article and English may not.

  • Generic or general reference (Me gusta el café, Los perros son leales)
  • Abstract nouns (La libertad es importante)
  • Languages as the subject of a sentence (El español es difícil, but not as object: Hablo español)
  • Repeated days of the week (Los lunes tengo clase)
  • Body parts/clothing with reflexive verbs (Me lavo las manos, Me quito los zapatos)
  • Telling time (Son las tres)
  • Titles when talking about someone (El señor García llegó, La doctora Martínez viene)
  • Superlatives (El mejor médico del hospital)
  • Identifying sentences with ser (when two things equal each other, e.g. Juan es el médico del pueblo)

When not to use the definite article

Here are some contexts where the definite article can be omitted in Spanish.

  • Classifying ser + noun/nationality/profession/religion, unmodified (when you categorize someone or something into a group without using extra descriptive adjectives, e.g. Es mentira, Es mexicano, Es médica)
  • Days of the week stated with ser (Hoy es lunes)
  • Languages as the direct object of hablar/estudiar/aprender (Hablo español)
  • Titles when addressing someone directly, or with don/doña/san/santo (Buenos días, doctor; Doña María llegó; Día de San Valentín)

Non-binary Definite Articles and Nouns

You'll encounter non-binary definite articles when referring to people who identify as non-binary or as genders outside of the masculine-feminine binary. While we've presented the non-binary definite articles of "le" and "les" here, the people you communicate with may use different forms. Gender inclusive language is very inventive, and speakers haven't settled on one "correct" form.

A common example used to illustrate gender inclusive language is "les amigues" to refer to friends who are gender non-conforming. However, noun invention is much richer than this. Strategies for forming non-binary nouns for people include adding an -e suffix to the end of the noun (e.g. ingeniero, ingeniera, ingeniere), and people are inventing the non-binary form for common nouns within their communities. For example, this article proposes "neidre", "adre", "hadre", and "edre" as non-binary forms for "madre" and "padre".
